We ran a within-subjects summative study evaluating SonoAdapt, a context-adaptive notification system for smart glasses (Meta Ray-Ban) that decides, per notification, whether to deliver it as an earcon, as spoken (TTS) audio, or suppress it entirely — based on a utility function derived from an earlier formative online study (9 scenarios, Taguchi T9 design, varying soundscape, social context, and mental/physical load).

The summative study compared SonoAdapt against two constant baselines: Baseline-Earcon (always plays an earcon) and Baseline-Speech (always reads the message aloud). Each participant experienced two task scenarios — an Interview task (participant is actively speaking, being asked questions) and a Math/Bill-checking task (participant silently verifies a total) — chosen because they stress different aspects of the system (speech interruption cost while talking vs. appropriateness of speech in a quiet, low-load context). A planned third scenario (changing batteries while music plays, to test earcon detectability under a music soundscape) was ultimately dropped to keep session length and required sample size manageable.

Each participant completed 12 trials: 2 scenarios × 3 systems (Earcon, Speech, SonoAdapt) × 2 urgency/importance levels (Low, High), with 6 unique LOW and 6 unique HIGH messages assigned so each message appears exactly once per person. Trial order was counterbalanced using Williams squares (scenario order alternated by participant parity; condition order balanced for position and first-order carry-over across 24 participants).

After each trial, participants rated Appropriateness, Detectability, Disruptiveness, and Social Acceptability on Likert scales (ands also the urgency/importance). Notifications were displayed as text on a printed dummy phone, which participants could flip over afterward to check the underlying message content.
