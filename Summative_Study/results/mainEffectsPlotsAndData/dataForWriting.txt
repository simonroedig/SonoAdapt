%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Statistical Approach and Interpretation}
\label{sec:statistical_approach}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

All four dependent variables (appropriateness, social acceptability, detectability, and disruptiveness) were measured on 7-point Likert scales. Because our design is fully within-subjects (3~systems $\times$ 2~urgency/importance levels $\times$ 2~scenarios), we first averaged each participant's four trials per system (2~scenarios $\times$ 2~urgency/importance levels) into a single score per system. This participant-level mean serves as the unit of analysis, collapsing across scenario and urgency/importance.

Since Likert responses are ordinal and several conditions exhibit ceiling or floor effects, we tested the main effect of system using the Friedman test, a non-parametric repeated-measures alternative to the one-way ANOVA that does not assume normality. Kendall's~$W$ is reported as the omnibus effect size. Values near~0 indicate no agreement among participants and~1 indicates perfect agreement in their rankings of the three systems.

For post-hoc pairwise comparisons, we conducted Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm-Bonferroni correction applied across the three comparisons within each measure. We report the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation~$r_{rb}$ as the pairwise effect size, ranging from $-1$ to $+1$, indicating the proportion of pairs favouring one condition over the other. All reported $p$-values are Holm-adjusted unless otherwise noted.

One important caveat concerns detectability. SonoAdapt suppresses low-urgency/low-importance notifications entirely (i.e., no sound is played), so participants could not meaningfully rate the detectability of those trials. To avoid confounding system with urgency, as SonoAdapt would otherwise only have high-urgency trials while the baselines include both, the detectability comparison is restricted to the high-urgency/high-importance trials, in which all three systems delivered an audible notification.


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{SonoAdapt vs Speech and Earcon}
\label{sec:sonoadapt_vs_speech_earcon}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Figure~\ref{fig:main_effects} summarises the ratings across all four dependent variables. The system had a significant main effect on appropriateness, social acceptability, and disruptiveness, all with large effect sizes ($W \geq 0.70$). No meaningful differences emerged for detectability.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{summativePlots/fig_mainEffects_SonoAdapt_4DV.png}
  \caption{Likert ratings by system for all four measures (participant-level means, $N = 23$, scale 1--7). Boxes show the median and interquartile range, whiskers span the full data range. Higher is better for all measures except disruptiveness. Brackets indicate Holm-corrected pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests ($^{*}p < .05$, $^{**}p < .01$, $^{***}p < .001$). Detectability is based on high-urgency/high-importance trials only, in which all three systems delivered an audible notification.}
  \label{fig:main_effects}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Appropriateness.}
The system significantly affected how appropriate the notification delivery was perceived ($\chi^{2}(2) = 32.11$, $p < .001$, $W = 0.70$). SonoAdapt was rated most appropriate ($M = 5.82$, $SD = 0.63$), significantly above both Earcon ($M = 4.49$, $SD = 0.86$, $p < .001$, $r_{rb} = -0.95$) and Speech ($M = 3.65$, $SD = 0.61$, $p < .001$, $r_{rb} = -0.99$). Earcon was in turn rated significantly more appropriate than Speech ($p = .003$, $r_{rb} = 0.76$).

\paragraph{Social Acceptability.}
The same ordering held for social acceptability ($\chi^{2}(2) = 37.06$, $p < .001$, $W = 0.81$). SonoAdapt was rated most socially acceptable ($M = 6.11$, $SD = 0.60$), above both Earcon ($M = 5.01$, $SD = 0.92$, $p < .001$, $r_{rb} = -0.98$) and Speech ($M = 4.01$, $SD = 0.61$, $p < .001$, $r_{rb} = -1.00$). Earcon again exceeded Speech ($p < .001$, $r_{rb} = 0.88$).

\paragraph{Disruptiveness.}
Systems differed significantly in perceived disruptiveness ($\chi^{2}(2) = 39.19$, $p < .001$, $W = 0.85$). SonoAdapt was rated least disruptive ($M = 2.64$, $SD = 0.71$), significantly below Earcon ($M = 3.51$, $SD = 1.13$, $p < .001$, $r_{rb} = 0.80$) and Speech ($M = 5.62$, $SD = 0.94$, $p < .001$, $r_{rb} = 1.00$). Speech was the most disruptive of the three, exceeding Earcon ($p < .001$, $r_{rb} = -1.00$).

\paragraph{Detectability.}
When a notification was actually delivered (in SonoAdapt for high-urgency/high-importance trials only), the Friedman test reached nominal significance ($\chi^{2}(2) = 6.92$, $p = .031$, $W = 0.15$), but with a very small effect size and no pairwise comparison surviving Holm correction (all $p_{Holm} \geq .079$). All three systems were highly detectable, with means above~5.9 on the 7-point scale (Earcon: $M = 5.96$, $SD = 1.08$, Speech: $M = 6.41$, $SD = 0.87$, SonoAdapt: $M = 6.33$, $SD = 0.56$). SonoAdapt's context-aware adaptation therefore does not come at the cost of reduced detectability for the notifications it does deliver.

\paragraph{Mute Baseline.}
To rule out a fully silent baseline, we additionally asked a subset of participants post-experimentally ($n = 17$) to rate the appropriateness of muting high-urgency/high-importance messages on the same 7-point scale. Ratings confirmed that withholding urgent notifications auditorily would be perceived as highly inappropriate ($M = 1.38$, $Mdn = 1.0$), far below all three tested systems. Combined with the finding that SonoAdapt's suppression of low-urgency/low-importance notifications was rated as highly appropriate ($M = 6.15$), this validates the system's selective strategy: suppress when urgency/importance is low, deliver auditorily when urgency/importance is high.
