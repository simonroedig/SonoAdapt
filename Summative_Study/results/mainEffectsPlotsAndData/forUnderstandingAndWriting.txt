─── EASY-TO-UNDERSTAND SUMMARY ────────────────────────────────────────────────

"Overall, which notification system do users prefer?"
We collapse everything (both scenarios, both urgency levels) and compare:
Earcon vs Speech vs SonoAdapt.
→ SonoAdapt wins on Appropriateness & Social Acceptability.
→ Speech is rated worst overall.
→ Disruptiveness: SonoAdapt is least disruptive (it mutes LOW notifications).
→ Detectability: handled specially because SonoAdapt mutes LOW (see below).

This is the analysis your supervisor primarily wants for the paper.


─── WHAT IS A "MAIN EFFECT"? ──────────────────────────────────────────────────

In ANOVA terminology, a "main effect" is the overall influence of one
independent variable (here: System) on the dependent variable, ignoring
(collapsing across) all other factors.

Our study has 3 factors:
  • System:              Earcon / Speech / SonoAdapt   (3 levels)
  • Urgency/Importance:  Low / High                    (2 levels)
  • Scenario:            Interview / Math               (2 levels)

The MAIN EFFECT OF SYSTEM collapses across Urgency and Scenario.
Each participant's score = mean of all 4 trials for that system
  (Interview-Low, Interview-High, Math-Low, Math-High).
This gives N=23 participants × 3 systems = 69 data points per DV.


─── WHAT WE COMPARE ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Three notification systems, fully within-subjects (every participant
experienced all three):
  1. Earcon:     short non-speech sound (always played)
  2. Speech:     spoken notification (always played)
  3. SonoAdapt:  adaptive system that decides to play earcon, speech, or mute
                 depending on context (scenario + urgency)

For the paper, SonoAdapt is called "AudioAdapt."


─── WHAT DATA WAS USED ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Source:         raw.xlsx (Qualtrics export, wide format)
Participants:   N = 23 (after removing 4 outliers from original 27)
Outliers:       Defined in potential_outliers.json
                Removed: Tejaswini, Ritika, Anna, Osama
Pilot:          First row removed (pilot participant)

Unit of analysis:
  Each participant's MEAN across all their trials for a given system.
  Per system, each participant has 4 trials (2 scenarios × 2 urgency levels).
  → 1 aggregated value per participant per system per DV.

Special case — Detectability:
  SonoAdapt mutes LOW-urgency notifications (by design). Users cannot rate
  detectability of something silent → those ratings are set to NaN.
  For Detectability, SonoAdapt's mean is computed from HIGH-only trials (2 per
  participant), while Earcon and Speech use all 4 trials each.
  This is transparently reported and defensible: you cannot detect silence.


─── DEPENDENT VARIABLES (DVs) ─────────────────────────────────────────────────

All are 7-point Likert scales:

  1. Appropriateness       (1=completely inappropriate … 7=completely appropriate)
                           ↑ higher = better
  2. Social Acceptability  (1=completely unacceptable  … 7=completely acceptable)
                           ↑ higher = better
  3. Detectability         (1=very difficult to detect … 7=very easy to detect)
                           ↑ higher = better
  4. Disruptiveness        (1=not disruptive at all    … 7=extremely disruptive)
                           ↓ LOWER = better (reversed scale!)

Plots come in two versions:
  • 4-DV version (includes Detectability)
  • 3-DV version (excludes Detectability) — because the Detectability
    comparison for SonoAdapt is imperfect (only HIGH trials contribute).


─── STATISTICAL TESTS USED ────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHY NON-PARAMETRIC?
  Likert data is ordinal. With N=23 and 7-point scales, normality assumptions
  for parametric tests (repeated-measures ANOVA) are questionable. Non-
  parametric tests are the conservative, safe choice that reviewers accept
  for ordinal Likert data with small samples.

OMNIBUS TEST:  Friedman test
  • Non-parametric equivalent of one-way repeated-measures ANOVA.
  • Tests: "Is there ANY difference among the 3 systems?"
  • Reports: χ²(2), p-value.
  • Effect size: Kendall's W (0 = no agreement, 1 = perfect agreement).
    W > 0.5 is considered a large effect.

POST-HOC PAIRWISE TESTS:  Wilcoxon signed-rank test
  • Non-parametric paired comparison (like a paired t-test but on ranks).
  • 3 pairwise comparisons: Earcon–Speech, Earcon–SonoAdapt, Speech–SonoAdapt.
  • Multiple comparison correction: Holm-Bonferroni method.
    (Controls family-wise error rate; less conservative than Bonferroni,
     more powerful, but still rigorous.)
  • Effect sizes reported:
    - r_rb: matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation (effect size for Wilcoxon)
    - r = |Z|/√N: standardized effect size
    - Cohen's d_z: paired Cohen's d (mean difference / SD of differences)
  • Significance threshold: α = .025 (from a-priori power analysis for 2
    scenario-level analyses; Bonferroni-adjusted from .05).

WHY NOT a linear mixed model (LMM)?
  LMMs assume interval-level data and normally distributed residuals. With
  ordinal 7-point Likert data and N=23, these assumptions are hard to defend.
  The Friedman + Wilcoxon approach is more appropriate for our data type and
  is the standard in HCI research with Likert scales.

─── WHAT THE SCRIPT DOES UNDER THE HOOD ───────────────────────────────────────

  1. Loads raw.xlsx (wide-format Qualtrics data)
  2. Removes the pilot row
  3. Reshapes wide → long format (1 row per trial)
  4. Maps Likert text labels to numeric 1–7 values
  5. Removes outlier participants (from potential_outliers.json)
  6. Sets Detectability = NaN for SonoAdapt + LOW trials
  7. Computes participant-level means (collapse across Scenario × Urgency)
  8. For each DV:
     a. Prints descriptive statistics (trial-level and participant-level)
     b. Runs Friedman omnibus test
     c. Runs 3 pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests
     d. Applies Holm-Bonferroni correction
     e. Computes effect sizes (r_rb, r, Cohen's d_z)
  9. Generates publication-ready boxplots (4 variants × 2 formats)
 10. Saves participantMeans.csv


─── REVIEWER DEFENSE NOTES ────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Q: "Why not use parametric tests (rm-ANOVA)?"
A: Our DVs are ordinal Likert scales (7-point). With N=23, normality of the
   sampling distribution is not guaranteed. Non-parametric tests (Friedman +
   Wilcoxon) make no distributional assumptions and are the standard approach
   for ordinal data in HCI research.

Q: "Why Holm-Bonferroni and not regular Bonferroni?"
A: Holm-Bonferroni controls the family-wise error rate (same guarantee as
   Bonferroni) but is uniformly more powerful. It is strictly better.

Q: "Why exclude Detectability for SonoAdapt LOW?"
A: SonoAdapt mutes LOW-urgency notifications by design. Users rated
   Detectability = "neither easy nor difficult" (value 4) because there was
   nothing to detect. Including these as real ratings would artificially lower
   SonoAdapt's Detectability score, which is misleading. The value "4" does
   not represent actual difficulty — it represents absence of a stimulus.
   This is transparently reported and all plots offer a 3-DV variant that
   excludes Detectability entirely.

Q: "Collapsing across Urgency and Scenario — is that valid?"
A: This is the standard main-effect analysis. It answers the highest-level
   question: "Overall, which system do users prefer?" The simple effects
   (in simpleEffects.py) then decompose this by urgency level, and the
   further effects (furtherEffects.py) decompose by scenario. The main effect
   is the starting point; follow-up analyses explain where the effect lives.

Q: "N=23 — is that enough?"
A: An a-priori power analysis (G*Power, Friedman test, 3 groups, α = .025,
   power = .80, medium effect size) indicated N ≥ 21 participants. We exceed
   this threshold. The observed effect sizes (Kendall's W > 0.5 for most DVs)
   confirm adequate power.
