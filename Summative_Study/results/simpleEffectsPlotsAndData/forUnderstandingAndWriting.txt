─── EASY-TO-UNDERSTAND SUMMARY ────────────────────────────────────────────────

"Does the system preference change depending on urgency/importance?"

We split the data into LOW and HIGH urgency/importance and repeat the
3-system comparison (Earcon vs Speech vs SonoAdapt) within each level.

→ LOW urgency:   SonoAdapt (muted) is overwhelmingly preferred.
                  Speech is rated worst (inappropriate for low-urgency).
→ HIGH urgency:  SonoAdapt ≈ best baseline. All three produce audible
                  notifications, differences are smaller.

These analyses explain WHERE the main effect comes from.


─── WHAT IS A "SIMPLE EFFECT"? ────────────────────────────────────────────────

A "simple effect" tests one factor (System) at a FIXED level of another
factor (Urgency/Importance). It is the standard follow-up when you have
a multi-factor design and want to decompose the main effect.

  Main effect:    System, collapsed across everything
  Simple effect:  System at LOW only  +  System at HIGH only

This answers: "Is the system preference the same for low-urgency and
high-urgency notifications, or does it differ?"


─── WHAT WE COMPARE ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Analysis 1 — LOW URGENCY/IMPORTANCE:
  Compare Earcon vs Speech vs SonoAdapt for LOW notifications only.
  Each participant's score = mean of their 2 LOW trials per system
    (Interview-Low + Math-Low).
  Key: SonoAdapt MUTES all LOW notifications → Detectability = NaN.

Analysis 2 — HIGH URGENCY/IMPORTANCE:
  Compare Earcon vs Speech vs SonoAdapt for HIGH notifications only.
  Each participant's score = mean of their 2 HIGH trials per system
    (Interview-High + Math-High).
  Key: SonoAdapt plays Earcon in Interview-High, Speech in Math-High.
  All systems produce audible output → all 4 DVs available.


─── WHAT DATA WAS USED ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Same as main effects:
  Source:       raw.xlsx
  N = 23       (after removing 4 outliers)
  Outliers:    Defined in potential_outliers.json

Unit of analysis:
  Per participant: mean of their 2 trials per system WITHIN a given
  urgency level. → 1 value per participant per system per DV per urgency.

Special case — Detectability for LOW:
  SonoAdapt mutes LOW → Detectability is NaN for SonoAdapt.
  → Friedman runs on 3 DVs only (Appropriateness, Social, Disruptiveness).
  → For Detectability-LOW, we report a 2-system Wilcoxon (Earcon vs Speech).
  This is the exact same logic as mainEffects.py, just more explicit here
  because we're already split by urgency.


─── STATISTICAL TESTS ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Identical pipeline to mainEffects.py:
  Omnibus:   Friedman test (χ²(2), Kendall's W)
  Post-hoc:  3 pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank, Holm-Bonferroni corrected
  Effect sizes: r_rb, r, Cohen's d_z
  α = .025 (from power analysis)

For Detectability-LOW (only 2 groups: Earcon vs Speech):
  Single Wilcoxon signed-rank test (no correction needed, only 1 comparison).

WHY SAME TESTS AS MAIN EFFECTS?
  Consistency. The simple effects use the same non-parametric approach so
  results are directly comparable. The only difference is the data subset.

─── WHAT THE SCRIPT DOES UNDER THE HOOD ───────────────────────────────────────

  1. Loads raw.xlsx and removes pilot row
  2. Reshapes wide → long (same as mainEffects.py)
  3. Maps Likert labels → numeric 1–7
  4. Removes outlier participants
  5. Sets Detectability = NaN for SonoAdapt + LOW
  6. SPLITS data into LOW and HIGH subsets
  7. For each urgency level:
     a. Computes participant-level means per system
     b. Runs Friedman + Wilcoxon pipeline per DV
     c. Handles Detectability specially for LOW
  8. Generates publication-ready boxplots
  9. Saves participant means CSVs


─── RELATIONSHIP TO MAIN EFFECTS ──────────────────────────────────────────────

  mainEffects.py  →  "Overall, which system is preferred?"
  simpleEffects.py →  "Is the preference the same for LOW and HIGH?"

The simple effects DECOMPOSE the main effect. If the main effect shows
SonoAdapt > Earcon > Speech, the simple effects reveal:
  - For LOW: SonoAdapt ≫ Earcon ≫ Speech  (huge differences, driven by muting)
  - For HIGH: differences are smaller, SonoAdapt ≈ best baseline

This decomposition is important because SonoAdapt behaves very differently
for LOW (mutes) vs HIGH (plays earcon or speech). Collapsing across urgency
hides this distinction.


─── REVIEWER DEFENSE NOTES ────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Q: "Isn't comparing SonoAdapt (muted) to Earcon/Speech unfair for LOW?"
A: No — that IS the point. SonoAdapt's adaptive decision to mute LOW
   notifications is a feature, not a confound. The high Appropriateness
   ratings (M=6.15) confirm users found muting appropriate for low-urgency
   messages. We transparently exclude Detectability from this comparison.

Q: "Why not do a full 3×2 factorial ANOVA instead of separate simple effects?"
A: (1) Our data is ordinal, not interval, so parametric ANOVA is questionable.
   (2) The Detectability issue (NaN for SonoAdapt+LOW) breaks the balanced
   design needed for factorial analysis.
   (3) Your supervisor's approach — main effect + simple effects — is the
   standard way to report this in HCI. A formal interaction test would
   require ART (Aligned Rank Transform) repeated-measures, which is a
   separate analysis your supervisor mentioned doing in R.

Q: "For HIGH, SonoAdapt plays Earcon in one scenario and Speech in the other.
    Aren't you mixing apples and oranges?"
A: Each participant's SonoAdapt-HIGH score is the mean of (Interview-HIGH
   where SonoAdapt played Earcon) and (Math-HIGH where SonoAdapt played
   Speech). This is exactly how participants experienced it. The sanity checks
   (in furtherEffects.py) confirm SonoAdapt's ratings match the corresponding
   baselines in each scenario, validating that the adaptive choices were
   equivalent to the baselines.
