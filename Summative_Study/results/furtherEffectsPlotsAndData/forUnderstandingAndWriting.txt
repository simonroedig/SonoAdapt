FURTHER EFFECTS — forUnderstandingAndWriting.txt
================================================================================

─── EASY-TO-UNDERSTAND SUMMARY ────────────────────────────────────────────────

"Why do the system differences look the way they do?"

These are EXPLORATORY pairwise analyses that dig into the individual
cells of the 3 (System) × 2 (Urgency) × 2 (Scenario) design.
They answer four specific questions:

  Group 1: Does urgency/importance change the ratings for each system?
  Group 2: Does SonoAdapt produce the same ratings as the baseline it mimics?
  Group 3: In each scenario, was SonoAdapt's choice the better system?
  Group 4: Does the scenario (Interview vs Math) affect ratings?


─── WHAT MAKES THIS DIFFERENT FROM MAIN/SIMPLE EFFECTS ────────────────────────

  Main effects:    1 factor (System), 3 levels, Friedman + Holm pairwise
  Simple effects:  Same, but split by Urgency (2 separate analyses)
  Further effects: Individual CELL-LEVEL comparisons (2 conditions each)

Further effects are EXPLORATORY. They use uncorrected p-values (no
Holm correction across groups) because they are hypothesis-generating,
not confirmatory. This is standard practice — you report them as
exploratory and let the reader judge the pattern.


─── GROUP 1: URGENCY/IMPORTANCE EFFECT ────────────────────────────────────────

QUESTION: "Does urgency level change how each system is rated?"

WHAT WE COMPARE:
  Within each System × Scenario cell, compare Low vs High.
  4 comparisons:
    • Interview × Earcon:  Low vs High
    • Interview × Speech:  Low vs High
    • Math × Earcon:       Low vs High
    • Math × Speech:       Low vs High
  (SonoAdapt is excluded because LOW = muted and HIGH = earcon or speech,
   so comparing them would mix modality and urgency — not meaningful.)

DATA:
  Each participant has exactly 1 trial per cell → paired Wilcoxon directly
  on the single trial values (no averaging needed).

KEY FINDING:
  Speech shows a MASSIVE Low→High improvement in Appropriateness:
    Interview: 1.65 → 4.87 (p < .001)
    Math:      2.22 → 5.87 (p < .001)
  Earcon is much more stable — urgency barely changes the ratings.

INTERPRETATION:
  Speech is very context-dependent: terrible for low-urgency (disruptive,
  inappropriate) but acceptable for high-urgency. Earcon is more "neutral"
  across urgency levels. This is WHY SonoAdapt's decision to mute LOW
  notifications (instead of playing speech) is so effective.

PLOT: fig_urgencyEffect.png/.pdf
  2×2 grid (rows: Interview/Math, cols: Earcon/Speech).
  Blue = Low, Red = High. Stars show significant differences.


─── GROUP 2: SANITY CHECKS ────────────────────────────────────────────────────

QUESTION: "Does SonoAdapt produce the same ratings as the baseline system
           it resolves to?"

BACKGROUND:
  For HIGH urgency, SonoAdapt plays:
    • Earcon in the Interview scenario
    • Speech in the Math scenario
  If the system works correctly, SonoAdapt's ratings should be statistically
  indistinguishable from the corresponding baseline.

WHAT WE COMPARE:
  • Interview HIGH: Earcon vs SonoAdapt (which played Earcon)
  • Math HIGH:      Speech vs SonoAdapt (which played Speech)

DATA:
  1 trial per participant per condition → paired Wilcoxon.

KEY FINDING:
  ✅ ALL sanity checks PASS. No significant differences on any DV.
  All p-values > .15. SonoAdapt's adaptive output is perceived identically
  to the corresponding baseline.

WHY THIS MATTERS:
  This validates two things:
  1. The adaptive system works as designed (plays the right sound).
  2. It justifies using baseline data for the Group 3 analysis (see below).

PLOT: fig_sanityChecks_SonoAdapt.png/.pdf (and AudioAdapt variant)
  1×2 grid. Titles explicitly state what SonoAdapt resolves to:
    "Sanity: Interview HIGH — Earcon vs SonoAdapt (= Earcon)"
    "Sanity: Math HIGH — Speech vs SonoAdapt (= Speech)"


─── GROUP 3: SYSTEM CHOICE FOR HIGH ───────────────────────────────────────────

QUESTION: "Was SonoAdapt's adaptive choice (Earcon in Interview, Speech in
           Math) actually the BETTER option in each scenario?"

IMPORTANT — WHAT DATA IS USED:
  We compare baseline Earcon vs baseline Speech for HIGH in each scenario.
  We do NOT use SonoAdapt's data points here.

  WHY? Because the sanity checks (Group 2) already proved that SonoAdapt's
  ratings are identical to the baseline it resolves to. So comparing baseline
  Earcon vs baseline Speech effectively compares "what SonoAdapt chose" vs
  "what it didn't choose." Using baseline data is cleaner because:
    (a) Participants explicitly knew they were hearing "earcon" or "speech"
        in the baseline conditions (no adaptive system in between).
    (b) The sanity check validates this substitution.

  A REVIEWER MIGHT ASK: "Why not use SonoAdapt's actual data?"
  ANSWER: We first established via sanity checks that SonoAdapt ≈ Baseline
  (all p > .15). Therefore, baseline data is a valid and cleaner proxy.
  Using SonoAdapt's data directly would give near-identical results (as
  the sanity checks prove) but would introduce the additional variable of
  participants knowing it was the "adaptive system."

WHAT WE COMPARE:
  • Interview HIGH: Earcon vs Speech → SonoAdapt chose Earcon
  • Math HIGH:      Earcon vs Speech → SonoAdapt chose Speech

KEY FINDINGS:

  Interview — SonoAdapt correctly chose Earcon:
    • Disruptiveness: Earcon (3.13) vs Speech (5.78), p < .001 ***
      → Earcon is FAR less disruptive during an interview.
    • Social Acceptability: Earcon (5.43) vs Speech (4.43), p = .006 **
      → Earcon is more socially acceptable in a conversation.
    • Appropriateness: no significant difference (both ~5).
    → Earcon is the clear winner in Interview.

  Math — SonoAdapt correctly chose Speech:
    • Appropriateness: Speech (5.87) vs Earcon (4.70), p = .042 *
    • Detectability: Speech (6.78) vs Earcon (5.78), p = .002 **
    • Disruptiveness: Speech (5.30) vs Earcon (3.70), p < .001 ***
      (Speech is MORE disruptive, but the Appropriateness and Detectability
       advantages outweigh this — during a solo math task, being noticeable
       matters more than being unobtrusive.)
    → Speech is the better choice for Math (high detectability + appropriate).

PLOT: fig_systemChoice_SonoAdapt.png/.pdf (and AudioAdapt variant)
  1×2 grid. Titles clearly state which system was chosen:
    "Interview HIGH: Earcon (chosen by SonoAdapt) vs Speech"
    "Math HIGH: Earcon vs Speech (chosen by SonoAdapt)"


─── GROUP 4: SCENARIO EFFECT ──────────────────────────────────────────────────

QUESTION: "Does the scenario (Interview vs Math) influence the ratings
           for each system?"

WHAT WE COMPARE:
  Within each System × Urgency cell, compare Interview vs Math.
  6 comparisons:
    • Speech × High:     Interview vs Math
    • Speech × Low:      Interview vs Math
    • Speech × Combined: Interview vs Math (mean of Low+High per scenario)
    • Earcon × High:     Interview vs Math
    • Earcon × Low:      Interview vs Math
    • Earcon × Combined: Interview vs Math

DATA:
  1 trial per participant per cell (High/Low) or mean of 2 (Combined).

KEY FINDINGS:
  Speech is VERY scenario-dependent:
    • Appropriateness: better in Math than Interview (almost all p < .05)
    • Social Acceptability: dramatically better in Math (p < .001 for Low/Comb.)
    • Detectability: higher in Math (p < .05)
    → Speech in Interview is perceived as socially inappropriate (interrupting
      a conversation), but in Math (solo task) it's acceptable.

  Earcon is more consistent across scenarios:
    • Most DVs show no significant scenario difference.
    • Exception: Disruptiveness is slightly higher in Math (p < .05).

INTERPRETATION:
  This confirms WHY SonoAdapt's adaptive strategy is effective:
  it avoids speech in Interview (where speech is inappropriate) and
  uses speech in Math (where speech works well). The scenario effect
  for speech validates SonoAdapt's decision-making logic.

PLOT: fig_scenarioEffect.png/.pdf
  2×3 grid (rows: Speech/Earcon, cols: High/Low/Combined).
  Blue = Interview, Orange = Math.


─── ALL STATISTICAL TESTS IN THIS FILE ────────────────────────────────────────

ALL comparisons use:
  Test:         Wilcoxon signed-rank (2-condition, paired)
  Why:          Each comparison has exactly 2 conditions (e.g., Low vs High)
                → Friedman is not needed (Friedman requires ≥3 groups).
  Effect sizes: r_rb (rank-biserial), r (|Z|/√N), Cohen's d_z
  Correction:   NONE — these are exploratory analyses.
                P-values are uncorrected within each comparison.
                This is explicitly stated in all output.

WHY UNCORRECTED?
  These are exploratory, hypothesis-generating analyses. Applying strict
  corrections across 20+ comparisons would be overly conservative and
  would mask genuine patterns. The standard in HCI is to:
  1. Report main effects with correction (done in mainEffects.py)
  2. Report exploratory follow-ups with uncorrected p-values + explicit
     labeling as "exploratory"
  A reviewer accepts this as long as you don't claim confirmatory results.

─── WHAT THE SCRIPT DOES UNDER THE HOOD ───────────────────────────────────────

  1. Loads raw.xlsx, removes pilot, reshapes wide → long
  2. Maps Likert labels → numeric 1–7
  3. Removes outliers (same 4 as main/simple effects)
  4. Pivots to per-cell participant-level values
  5. For each group (1–4):
     a. Defines the comparison pairs
     b. Extracts participant-level data for each pair
     c. Runs Wilcoxon signed-rank test per DV
     d. Computes effect sizes
  6. Generates separate figures for each group
  7. Saves all results to furtherEffectsResults.txt


─── HOW ALL THREE ANALYSES FIT TOGETHER ───────────────────────────────────────

  LEVEL 1 — Main Effects (mainEffects.py):
    "Overall, which system is best?"
    → Confirmatory. Friedman + Holm-corrected pairwise. For the paper.

  LEVEL 2 — Simple Effects (simpleEffects.py):
    "Is the system preference the same for LOW and HIGH urgency?"
    → Semi-confirmatory. Same Friedman pipeline, split by urgency.

  LEVEL 3 — Further Effects (furtherEffects.py):
    "WHY do the differences look the way they do?"
    → Exploratory. Cell-level pairwise comparisons. Uncorrected.

─── REVIEWER DEFENSE NOTES ────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Q: "Why use baseline Earcon/Speech instead of SonoAdapt's data for Group 3?"
A: The sanity checks (Group 2) establish that SonoAdapt ≈ Baseline for all
   DVs (all p > .15). Baseline data provides a cleaner comparison because
   participants knew exactly which notification type they heard.

Q: "These are a lot of comparisons — aren't you p-hacking?"
A: These are explicitly labeled as exploratory analyses with uncorrected
   p-values. The confirmatory results are in mainEffects.py (with proper
   Holm-Bonferroni correction). The exploratory analyses help explain the
   patterns and validate the adaptive system's design decisions.

Q: "Why not include SonoAdapt in the urgency effect (Group 1)?"
A: SonoAdapt LOW = muted, SonoAdapt HIGH = earcon/speech. Comparing them
   would mix notification modality (silence vs sound) with urgency level.
   This is not a clean comparison and would be misleading.

Q: "The scenario effect (Group 4) — why no correction across 6 comparisons?"
A: Exploratory. The pattern (speech is scenario-dependent, earcon isn't) is
   consistent across urgency levels, which strengthens the finding even
   without formal correction.
